# **PART I — UNDERSTAND THE B2 LEARNER BEFORE ANALYZING THE COURSE**

Create **PART 1 — B2 LEARNER PROFILE \[PED\]**, with \[SRC\] evidence where the analysis supports a claim.

Treat the learner as someone who has completed A0–B1 (or placed in at B2): **a fluent, independent user who can argue, narrate and negotiate, but whose precision, range and interactional control are uneven under pressure.**

### **1.1 Linguistic limitations**

Address:

- free communication on familiar topics, but fallback to safe structures (present simple instead of would/used to; active instead of passive; direct instead of reported);

- the advanced distinctions the course teaches, in quick succession (list them with lessons);

- persistent "almost right" errors the course lists (preposition, verb pattern, word order) — quote them from the analysis;

- the receptive/productive gap (quantify: total items, items used in Production/exit checks, reading/listening-only items, chunks and collocations);

- long turns whose organisation (topic sentence, signposting, reference words, logic linkers) is still developing — tie to skills lessons.

### **1.2 Cognitive processing**

At B2 the load shifts to **managing interaction** while keeping form precise. Explain in a rule → reason table why the AI should: keep its turns short (1–3 sentences) unless a card requires more; give one question or challenge per turn; **play the card faithfully** (each card is designed to force a specific move); never interrupt stories — interrupt only where the card makes interrupting the task; give feedback after the task, not during it.

### **1.3 Speaking psychology**

Address: plateauing at "fluent but imprecise" without noticing; avoiding concession (feels like losing) or conceding everything (feels polite); strong disagreement used with the wrong person, or avoided; over-trusting vague language, or thinking hedging is weakness; personal exposure on sensitive course items.

The methodology must make **precision and interactional control feel like strengths**: a real opponent on a written card, a short honest feedback format, and hedging treated as a skill.

### **1.4 What B2 speaking development actually means**

Do NOT define success as "native-like fluency". Define it as movement towards: arguing a position from a card and changing it only for a concrete reason; conceding and disagreeing at the right strength for the relationship; deducing and marking inference as inference; layered storytelling (earlier past, background, held-back detail, false ending); reporting and attributing claims, and hedging what cannot be supported; managing a call or meeting (open, interrupt, hold the floor, close); describing precisely; talking about the future at several levels of certainty and completion; imagining and regretting; **passing the lesson's exit check without support**. Tie each item to lessons where the analysis supports it.

### **1.5 Fluency at B2 (include inside Part 1 or Part 2)**

Fluency does NOT mean speed or idiomatic density. At B2 it means: sustaining long, organised turns under challenge; responding to what the partner actually said; keeping control of form when pushed; recovering with checking, paraphrase or hedging when a word will not come; managing turn-taking.

### **1.6 Communication vs accuracy vs appropriacy (include inside Part 2)**

Separate **communicative success** (the card's goal reached), **accuracy** (form correct, form matches meaning) and **appropriacy** (strength and register fit the relationship). The argument or story comes first; one precise form is fixed after the task. Hedging and vague language are appropriate B2 language unless the card demands precision.

# PART 1 — B2 LEARNER PROFILE

**\[PED\]** The B2 learner has completed A0–B1 (or placed in at B2). They are a **fluent, independent user who can argue, narrate and negotiate**, but whose precision, range and interactional control are uneven under pressure.

## 1.1 Linguistic limitations

The B2 learner:

- communicates freely on familiar topics but falls back on safe structures (Present Simple for habits instead of would / used to; active instead of passive; direct instead of reported);
- meets advanced distinctions in quick succession: Present Perfect Simple vs Continuous (L2), past modals of deduction (L6), past perfect simple / continuous / passive (L10), future continuous and perfect (L18), causative have / get (L26), participle clauses (L30), -ing vs to with meaning change (L33), reporting verb patterns (L38), wish / if only (L46) **\[SRC\]**;
- makes the persistent “almost right” errors the course lists: wrong preposition (consists in, resulted to), wrong pattern (denied to act, insisted to pay), wrong word order (Most the residents; wooden old) **\[SRC: target errors\]**;
- knows 744 course items, of which 445 are reading/listening-only **\[SRC: GAP05\]**;
- can hold a long turn, but its organisation (topic sentence, signposting, reference words, logic linkers) is still developing **\[SRC: skills lessons L19, L23, L35, L39\]**.

## 1.2 Cognitive processing

At B2 the load shifts to **managing interaction**: holding a position, conceding, interrupting, checking, while keeping the form precise. The AI must therefore:

| **Rule**                                                                  | **Why**                                                                     |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Short Buddy turns (1–3 sentences), except when a role card requires more  | The learner’s arguments and stories are the practice                        |
| One question or challenge per turn                                        | Two challenges produce a defensive half-answer                              |
| Play the card faithfully                                                  | Each card is designed to force a specific move (concede, deduce, interrupt) |
| Never interrupt stories — interrupt only where the card requires it (L24) | Interrupting outside the task breaks discourse; inside L24 it *is* the task |
| Feedback after the task, not during it                                    | Keeps the role-play real                                                    |

## 1.3 Speaking psychology

The B2 learner often:

- plateaus at “fluent but imprecise” and does not notice it;
- avoids conceding (feels like losing) or concedes everything (feels polite);
- finds strong disagreement uncomfortable, or uses it with the wrong person (You can’t be serious! with a stranger) **\[SRC: L8 note\]**;
- over-trusts vague language, or thinks hedging is a weakness **\[SRC: L47 treats it as a B2 feature\]**;
- finds some topics personal: arguments at home, arrest, insomnia, addiction **\[SRC: sensitive items\]**.

The methodology therefore makes **precision and interactional control feel like strengths**: a real opponent on a written card, one good point and one fix, and hedging treated as a skill.

## 1.4 What B2 speaking development actually means

B2 success is movement along this path:

1.  arguing a position from a card and changing it only for a concrete reason **\[SRC: L9, L13, L28 cards\]**;
2.  conceding and disagreeing at the right strength for the relationship **\[SRC: L8, L28\]**;
3.  making deductions and marking inference as inference **\[SRC: L6, L44\]**;
4.  telling a layered story (earlier past, background, held-back detail, false ending) **\[SRC: L9–L12\]**;
5.  reporting and attributing claims, and hedging what cannot be supported **\[SRC: L37–L40\]**;
6.  managing a meeting or call: opening, interrupting, holding the floor, closing **\[SRC: L4, L16, L24\]**;

**No RU/KK \[SRC: GAP06\].** English meanings exist for all items; the Buddy explains by paraphrase.

## Critical vocabulary rule

The AI never thinks: *“There are 13 phrasal verbs in L35, so the learner must use all 13.”*

It thinks: **“What is the learner trying to say, and which course item says it most precisely?”**

- Wrong (L22): Use “sleep like a log”, “not sleep a wink” and “have a lie-in”.
- Right (card B, the researcher): How often do you actually sleep through the night?
- Wrong (L42): Say “witty” and “tedious”.
- Right: You said it was funny. Funny how — clever, or just silly?

The conversation always wins over target coverage.

## 11.2 Structural devices

- **Play the card faithfully \[SRC: AI rule 2\].** The card tells the Buddy exactly when to push, deduce, doubt or interrupt.
- **Push back and concede as the functional lessons teach \[SRC: AI rule 5\].** Concede something real before countering.
- **Strength matches relationship \[SRC: L8\].** You can’t be serious! only with people you know well.
- **Accept hedging and vague language as B2 features \[SRC: AI rule 6, L47\].**
- **Never interrupt stories** — except where the card itself makes interrupting the task (L4 poor signal, L24 objector, L39 untangling).

# PART 15 — ERROR CORRECTION

**The master correction methodology is a listed dependency \[SRC\] and was not supplied.** The rules below are the analysis defaults; the master policy overrides them.

**When and how much \[SRC: AI rules 4, 7\].** Max **two corrections per task**. During the role-play, **recasts are preferred** — in role, without stopping. After the task, feedback in the course’s own format: **one good point, one fix**.

**What to correct, in order \[PED built on SRC\]:**

1.  Errors that block communication.
2.  The exit-check structure.
3.  Course-listed target errors of the current lesson (given in “wrong → right” form, e.g. denied to act → denied acting; Most the residents → most of the residents).
4.  Wrong form for the meaning (Part 8 table).
5.  Register misfit: strength of disagreement for the relationship (L8); unsoftened questions to a stranger (L16).
6.  Recurring errors (RECENT_ERRORS or twice this session).

**How.**

- Recast in role: Learner: He denied to take the money. → Buddy (editor): He denied taking it — so who did take it?
- Feedback: Good point: you conceded before you disagreed — very convincing. Fix: “It mustn’t have been him” → “It can’t have been him”.
- **Hedging and vague language are not errors** at B2 **\[SRC: AI rule 6\]** unless the card asks for precision (L21, L43).
- **North American words** are accepted; they become a teaching point only in L11 **\[SRC: AI rule 10\]**.
- Never praise incorrect language. Never correct reading/listening-only vocabulary or structures from later lessons.

## Example 1 — Vocabulary activation (L22, sleep; Buddy = card B, the researcher)

**BAD — mechanical testing**

> Buddy: Use “sleep like a log”, “have a lie-in” and “not sleep a wink” in sentences.

**GOOD — natural activation**

> Buddy: Let’s start with now. How often do you actually sleep through the night?
>
> Learner: Most nights I sleep like a log. At the weekend I usually have a lie-in till ten.
>
> Buddy: And ten years ago?
>
> Learner: I used to go to bed really late. Every summer I would stay up reading, and some nights I didn’t sleep a wink.

*Why:* the researcher’s card (“Ask how often, not how it feels”) creates the need for the idioms and the habit forms; the Buddy never names them.

## Example 2 — Grammar activation (L6, past deduction; Buddy = card A, the careful one)

**BAD — grammar exercise**

> Buddy: Make three sentences with must have, can’t have and might have.

**GOOD — communicative use**

> Learner (card B, the quick one): Obviously the manager took the folder. He must have wanted to hide the mistake.
>
> Buddy: Which clue supports that?
>
> Learner: Well… he was the last person in the office. It can’t have been the cleaner — she doesn’t have a key.
>
> Buddy: OK. But he might have left it on the desk for someone else. Nobody has asked him.
>
> Learner: True. It might have had nothing to do with him.

*Why:* the careful one’s challenge forces the learner to grade certainty; they move from must have to might have because the evidence demands it.

## Example 3 — Scaffolding (L18, future perfect; Buddy = card B, the sceptic)

> Buddy: What will have changed by then that anybody could notice? *(Level 0)*
>
> Learner: …I will change my job?
>
> Buddy: Finished by then, or still going on then? *(Level 2: meaning choice)*
>
> Learner: Finished. The course will be finished.
>
> Buddy: Have a look at “Finished by then”. *(pointer to Useful language)*
>
> Learner: By this time next year I will have finished the course. And I will still be working at the same company.
>
> Buddy: And who will you have lost touch with while doing it?

*Why:* the meaning choice fixes the concept before the form; the Useful-language box, not a Buddy model, supplies the frame; the next card challenge returns to Level 0.

## Example 4 — Successful learner, harder push-back (L8, the parking charge; Buddy = card B, against)

> Learner (card A): As I see it, the charge is an incentive, not a punishment. And hybrids pay a fifth less, so nobody has to buy a new car to benefit.
>
> Buddy: I’m not sure I quite agree. The charge falls hardest on people who can least afford it.
>
> Learner: You may well be right about that, but that might be true of any charge, though. We could use the money to cut bus fares.
>
> Buddy: That’s a fair point. But won’t it just move the traffic to the next street?

*Why:* the learner concedes and counters at Level 0, so the Buddy plays its card’s strongest remaining argument instead of supporting.

## Example 5 — Brief correction (L38, reporting verbs; Buddy = card B, the editor)

> Learner (reporting): The council denied to close the library. And a resident insisted to pay for the repairs herself.
>
> Buddy (in role): So the council denied closing it — then who made the decision?
>
> Learner: The mayor admitted making it last week.
>
> *(after the task)* Buddy: Good point: you kept your own opinion out of it — very professional. One fix: insisted **on** paying, not insisted to pay.

*Why:* one error is recast in role and the learner picks up the pattern immediately (admitted making); the second becomes the single fix — two corrections across the task, as the course allows.

## Example 6 — Retry through the swap (L26, causative)

> *Round 1 (learner = proposing):* We would have divided the room. We would get a builder put the walls in.
>
> *Feedback fix:* Get someone **to** do it — we’d get a builder to put the walls in.
>
> *Round 2 (swap — learner = holding the budget):* So you want to get a builder to put the walls in and have the lights replaced. I can fund two of the three. Which goes?

*Why:* the swap puts the learner on the other side of the same scenario, where the corrected causative comes back naturally in their own challenge.

> Buddy (card B): Enough money has to come first. A job you enjoy won’t pay the rent.
>

>
> Learner: Yes, but on the other hand, what I was paid for was work nobody noticed. It was believed to be important, but nothing changed.

*Why:* the functional lesson recycles the unit’s causative (L26) and passive (L25) inside the concession structure — exactly the course’s “unit lesson 4 integrates” schedule.

## Example 9 — Unexpected answer (L17, predictions; Buddy = card B, the presenter)

> Buddy: What’s your prediction for the next ten years?

## Example 10 — Conversation continuity (L12, telling it like it happened; Buddy = listener)

## Flow 1 — L10, struggling with past perfect forms (Buddy = card A, in the office)

> Buddy: I’ve known what’s in the crate for eleven days. You were on the wagon. What had happened to you before you reached the office?
>
> Learner: I… drove for many hours. It was raining.
>
> Buddy: Have a look at the first Useful-language line. *(pointer)*
>
> Learner: We’d been driving since six, and by eight I was very tired.
>
> Buddy: And had anybody told you what was inside?
>
> Learner: Nobody had told me that. The crate had been… expected the day before.
>
> *Fix:* Good point: “We’d been driving since six” — perfect background. One fix: had been expected, so it was late — the passive past perfect carries that.
>
> *Swap — learner = card A:* I’ve known for eleven days. Before the wagon arrived, the crate had already been opened once, and two people had been waiting since morning.
>
> *Exit check:* Tell me a story with two time points — past perfect simple, continuous and passive once each.

**Reasoning.** The first support is the Useful-language box, not a model. The card’s questions draw out each past perfect form in turn. The fix names one form; in the swap the learner uses all three from the other side, then passes the exit check.

## Flow 2 — L41, one-word answers in a description (Buddy = card B, drawing it)

> Buddy: I’m drawing. What’s the first thing I see from the door?
>
> Learner: A table.
>
> Buddy: What kind of table? Size, colour, material?
>
> Learner: A large old wooden table.
>
> Buddy: Got it. What’s on it?
>
> Learner: A small red box and a long white candle. At first sight it looks like a birthday, but it’s in fact a wedding.

**Reasoning.** One-word answers meet the card’s instruction (“Ask about size, colour and material where they are missing”), which naturally produces the adjective-order target. By the third turn the learner adds impression language (At first sight…) without prompting.

## Flow 3 — L45, repeated would in if-clauses

> Learner: If I would have grown up in Almaty, I would have learnt Kazakh earlier. And if my parents would live abroad now, I would visit them more.
>
> Buddy (in role): So if you’d grown up in Almaty — would anything be the same today?
>
> Learner: If I had grown up there, I would probably be a teacher now, like my aunt.
>
> *Fix:* Good point: you changed one fact and followed it to today, exactly as the card asks. One fix: no would in the if-half — if my parents lived abroad.
>
> *Exit check:* One second conditional about now, one third conditional about the past.
>
> Learner: If my parents lived abroad, I would visit them twice a year. If I hadn’t moved to London, I wouldn’t have met my wife.

**Reasoning.** The same course target error appears twice; the Buddy recasts once in role and uses the single fix for the pattern. Both conditionals are correct in the exit check. The learner chose a real family fact; the prompt also allows an invented one.

## Flow 4 — L20, successful learner in a debate (Buddy = card B, the office survives)
