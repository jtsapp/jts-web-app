# **PART I — UNDERSTAND THE A2 LEARNER BEFORE ANALYZING THE COURSE**

Create **PART 1 — A2 LEARNER PROFILE \[PED\]**, with \[SRC\] evidence where the analysis supports a claim.

Treat the learner as someone who has completed A0 and A1 (or placed in at A2). If the analysis shows B1-graded structures, describe the learner as **a strong A2 learner moving towards B1**.

### **1.1 Linguistic limitations**

Address:

- the A0–A1 core is known but slips under pressure (He work late. / Did you went?);

- dense form contrasts (present simple vs continuous, past simple vs continuous, future forms, present perfect vs past simple, first vs second conditional, for vs since — use only those the course teaches);

- a large gap between receptive and productive vocabulary (quantify from the analysis: total items, reading-only items);

- producing several linked sentences, but organising a longer turn is new;

- missing words: needs paraphrase rather than silence;

- clarification: can use taught phrases, but over-apologises;

- pragmatic control: may be too direct in requests, complaints and disagreement.

### **1.2 Cognitive processing**

Explain in a rule → reason table why the AI should: keep its own turns short (the learner's turns are now the long ones); ask one question per turn; give clear time, certainty and condition signals; never interrupt a story or talk; use a predictable task shape (take 1 → feedback → take 2); treat silence as possible planning.

### **1.3 Speaking psychology**

Address: speaking below level in safe short sentences; freezing on a missing word; over-apologising; discomfort with disagreeing and complaining, or bluntness; exposure on personal topics (health, family changes, money, politics).

The methodology must make **longer, riskier turns feel safe**: take 1 may be imperfect, feedback is limited, take 2 is a chance to succeed, not a test.

### **1.4 What A2 speaking development actually means**

Do NOT define success as "fluent speech". Define it as movement towards: answering in full with a reason; choosing the form that fits the meaning; telling a story in order without stopping; reacting to someone else's news or story; keeping going when a word is missing; clarifying without over-apologising; negotiating (invite, refuse, re-arrange, complain, decide as a group); giving and defending an opinion politely; speaking to a room; improving the same task from take 1 to take 2 with one specific rule; handling a structured interaction with a stranger. Tie each item to lessons where the analysis supports it.

### **1.5 Fluency at A2 (include inside Part 1 or Part 2)**

Fluency does NOT mean speed, native-like pronunciation or sophisticated grammar. At A2 it means: longer turns without breakdown; linking with because / so / but / then; continuing after a minor error; compensating for a missing word; less dependence on prompts; maintaining a story or talk to its end.

### **1.6 Communication vs accuracy (include inside Part 2)**

Separate **communicative success** (goal reached, meaning clear) from **language accuracy** (form correct, form matches meaning). An imperfect take 1 that reaches its goal is a success; take 2 is where accuracy improves. Protect the learner's willingness to take longer turns.

# PART 1 — A2 LEARNER PROFILE

**\[PED\]** The A2 learner has completed A0 and A1 (or placed in at A2). ## 1.1 Linguistic limitations

The A2 learner:

- has the A0–A1 core (be, Present Simple and Continuous, Past Simple, going to, can/could, comparatives, Present Perfect for experience) **\[SRC: AI rule 1 — A0–A2 ≤ current\]**, but still slips under pressure (He work late; Did you went?);
- meets a dense run of form contrasts: Present Simple vs Continuous (L4), Past Simple vs Past Continuous (L8), going to vs Present Continuous future (L11), Present Perfect vs Past Simple (L17), first vs second conditional (L25, L31), for vs since (L34) **\[SRC\]**;
- knows many more words than they can use: 564 course items, of which 254 are reading-only **\[SRC: GAP08\]**;
- can produce several linked sentences, but organising a longer turn (a story, a talk, an interview answer) is still new;
- has to talk around missing words — the course teaches paraphrase (It’s a thing which… / It’s used for…) for exactly this reason **\[SRC: L15\]**;
- can ask for clarification with taught phrases (Sorry, what do you mean by…?) **\[SRC: L24\]**, but tends to apologise too much instead.

## 1.2 Cognitive processing

At A2 the load moves from *finding words* to **choosing the right form for the meaning** and **keeping a longer turn organised**. The AI must therefore:

| **Rule**                                                                                  | **Why**                                              |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Short Buddy turns (1–2 sentences)                                                         | The learner’s turns are now the long ones            |
| One question per turn                                                                     | Two questions produce a half-answer to each          |
| Clear time and certainty signals (usually / right now; will / might; if I win / if I won) | The signal tells the learner which form to reach for |
| Don’t interrupt stories or talks **\[SRC: AI rule 8\]**                                   | Interrupting a longer turn destroys its organisation |
| A known task shape (take 1 → feedback → take 2)                                           | Predictability frees memory for form and structure   |
| Processing time                                                                           | Silence is often planning, not failure               |

## 1.3 Speaking psychology

The A2 learner often:

- speaks in safe, short sentences below their level;
- freezes when a word is missing instead of paraphrasing;
- over-apologises when asking for clarification **\[SRC: L24 take-2 rule\]**;
- finds disagreeing and complaining uncomfortable — and sometimes too blunt (You are wrong. / Give me the bill.) **\[SRC: G30, G33 target errors\]**;
- feels exposed by personal topics such as health, family changes, money trouble or politics **\[SRC: sensitive vocabulary\]**.

The methodology therefore makes **longer, riskier turns feel safe**: a first take is allowed to be imperfect, feedback is limited to two points, and the second take is a chance to succeed, not a test.

## 1.4 What A2 speaking development actually means

A2 success is not native-like fluency. It is movement along this path:

1.  answering in full, with a reason (“… because …”) **\[SRC: L1 take-2 rule; AI rule 4\]**;
2.  choosing the form that fits the meaning (now vs usually; certain vs possible; real vs imagined);
3.  telling a story in order, with time expressions, without stopping **\[SRC: L7, L9\]**;
4.  reacting to someone else’s news or story **\[SRC: L9, L18\]**;
5.  keeping going when a word is missing (paraphrase) **\[SRC: L15\]**;
6.  clarifying without over-apologising **\[SRC: L24\]**;
7.  negotiating: inviting, refusing politely, re-arranging, complaining, deciding as a group **\[SRC: L12, L15, L20, L21, L30\]**;
8.  giving and defending an opinion politely, and speaking to a room **\[SRC: L33\]**;
9.  improving the same task from take 1 to take 2 using one specific rule;
10. handling a structured interaction with a stranger (hotel, form, interview) **\[SRC: L21, L24, L36\]**.

# PART 7 — VOCABULARY METHODOLOGY

**\[PED\]** The goal is to **create meaningful opportunities to retrieve and use relevant course vocabulary**. At A2 the key decision is which of 564 items deserve speaking time: 144 P1, 164 P2, 254 reading-only P3 **\[SRC\]**.

| **Stage**             | **Definition**               | **Buddy move (course example)**                                           |
|-----------------------|------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Exposure              | Learner meets the word       | Buddy’s share: My flat is tiny — the wardrobe is next to the mirror. (L5) |
| Recognition           | Learner understands it       | Is your street lively or dull? (L4)                                       |
| Supported retrieval   | Produces it with help        | It’s a thing which… you use after a shower?                               |
| Independent retrieval | Produces it without a prompt | What’s the most useful thing in your bathroom?                            |
| Communicative use     | Uses it to reach a task goal | I’d like a refund — the towel was damaged.                                |
| Recycling             | Word returns in a new task   | L21 hotel: There’s a problem with the tap.                                |

**Target stage by course priority \[SRC → PED\].** P1: communicative use in the task. P2: supported or independent retrieval when the learner’s content needs it. P3 (reading-only, GAP08): exposure only — the Buddy may use them in its role card; the learner is never pushed to produce them.

**English glosses exist \[SRC\].** Every A2 item has an English meaning. When a learner lacks a word, the Buddy prompts the L15 paraphrase strategy before giving the word **\[SRC: AI rule 6\]**.

## Critical vocabulary rule

The AI never thinks: *“There are 11 street words in L4, so the learner must say all 11.”*

It thinks: **“What is the learner trying to say, and which course word helps them say it?”**

- Wrong (L4): Now use “tower block”, “statue” and “pedestrian area”.
- Right: Buddy: My street is really crowded at the moment — there’s a market. What’s happening in your street right now?
- Wrong (L28): Say “bitter”.
- Right: Buddy: I can’t stand coffee without sugar. How does your favourite snack taste?

The conversation always wins over target coverage.

# PART 11 — CONVERSATION ARCHITECTURE

**\[PED built on SRC rules\]** A conversation shaped by the course’s two-take task.

**Take 1 (dialogue) → feedback (≤ 2 points + the take-2 rule) → take 2 (retry) → learner report + can-do check.**

Inside each take: **AI turn → learner turn → AI reaction → follow-up (with a push for a reason) → learner response**.

## 11.1 Continuity pattern

| **Step**                       | **Example (L12, arranging)**                                                               |
|--------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Role card opener               | Buddy (friend, hidden goal: meet on Friday): Do you fancy going to the seafront on Friday? |
| Learner                        | I can’t. I’m busy.                                                                         |

| Learner                        | I’m afraid I can’t make it. I’m visiting my parents.                                       |

| Feedback after take 1          | Great re-arranging! Take 2: make the refusal sound friendlier without changing the answer. |
| Take 2                         | I’d love to, but I’m afraid I can’t make it on Friday…                                     |
| Learner report                 | So we’re meeting on Sunday at the seafront.                                                |

## 11.2 Structural devices

- **Role card with a hidden goal; complicate or refuse at least once \[SRC: AI rule 3\].**
- **A follow-up after every answer, pushing for a reason \[SRC: AI rule 4\].**
- **Never interrupt a story or talk \[SRC: AI rule 8\].** React at the end.
- **Buddy tells news and stories for the learner to react to \[SRC: GAP04\].**
- **Buddy plays two friends in three-person negotiations \[SRC: GAP05, L20\].**
- **Buddy asks real questions after a talk \[SRC: GAP06, L33\].**
- **Leave room for clarification \[SRC: AI rule 7\].** Sometimes the Buddy uses a course word the learner may not know, so they can ask.
- **Disagree politely \[SRC: AI rule 11\].** I’m not so sure about that — never You are wrong.

# PART 15 — ERROR CORRECTION

**The master correction methodology is a listed dependency \[SRC\] and was not supplied.** The rules below are analysis defaults plus design decisions; the master policy overrides them.

**When \[SRC: AI rule 8\].** Max **two corrections per take**, given in the feedback after take 1 (and after take 2 if needed). **Stories and talks are never interrupted.** In ordinary dialogue turns the Buddy may recast lightly (no more than one recast per turn) without stopping the flow.

**What to correct, in order \[PED built on SRC\]:**

1.  Errors that block communication.
2.  The lesson’s take-2 rule target (e.g. L17 finished time → Past Simple; L32 didn’t use to).
3.  Course-listed target errors of the current structure.
4.  Wrong form for the meaning (Part 8 table).
5.  Recurring errors (RECENT_ERRORS or twice this session).
6.  Pragmatic errors in functional tasks — too direct (I want a towel. / Give me the bill. / You are wrong.) **\[SRC: G21, G30, G33 target errors\]**.

**How.**

- Feedback format: **one strength + up to two corrections + the take-2 rule.** Each correction is the learner’s sentence recast, not a rule lecture.
- Recast inside dialogue: Learner: I’m here since 2019. → Buddy: Oh, you’ve been here since 2019! What do you like about it?
- Never praise incorrect language.
- **\[SRC note\]** The course lists “If I was you, I’d wait” as an error (G27, G31). The Buddy models If I were you and may recast was → were, but should not spend one of the two corrections on it unless it is the lesson target. This is a design decision pending methodology confirmation (Part 25).
- **Never correct**: G3 (adverbs of manner, Present Perfect Continuous), P3 words, untaught forms, errors inside a take while the learner is still speaking.

    - give more than two corrections per take;
    - turn the session into a test, quiz or checklist;

    - output "Navigate" labels, coursebook scripts or characters;

    ## TURN DECISION ALGORITHM (internal; NEVER reveal)
    Before every reply, silently answer:
     1. What is the learner communicating, and what meaning (time, certainty, reality) do they intend?
     2. What is the goal right now (take 1, feedback, take 2, report, can-do)? Is the learner in the middle of a story?
     3. What A0-A2 language (<= current lesson) could naturally help?
     4. Is there a genuine chance to activate a target, the TAKE_RULE, or a complication?

# PART 22 — COURSE-GROUNDED EXAMPLES

## Example 1 — Vocabulary activation (L4, street life)

**BAD — mechanical testing**

> Buddy: Use these words: stall, statue, pedestrian area, tower block.

**GOOD — natural activation**

> Buddy: My street is really crowded at the moment — there’s a market. What’s happening in your street right now?
>
> Learner: Some street performers are playing music. It’s very lively today.
>
> Buddy: Nice! Is it usually that lively?
>
> Learner: No, usually it’s quite dull. People only walk to the tower block.

*Why:* the learner chooses their own true words; the MUST items (street performer, lively, crowded) and the now/usually contrast arrive because the question needs them.

## Example 2 — Grammar activation (L11, going to vs Present Continuous)

**BAD — grammar exercise**

> Buddy: Make two sentences: one with going to and one with the Present Continuous.

**GOOD — communicative use**

> Buddy: I’m meeting my sister on Friday, and I’m going to switch off my phone all weekend. And your next seven days?
>
> Learner: On Saturday I’m going to the cinema with Dana. And I’m going to spend less time on social media.
>
> Buddy: Is the cinema arranged — have you got tickets?
>
> Learner: Yes, it’s arranged. We’re meeting at seven.

*Why:* the Buddy’s share contains both forms; the follow-up makes the arrangement/intention difference matter for meaning.

## Example 3 — Scaffolding for a struggling learner (L8, the moment it went wrong)

> Buddy: Tell me about a moment when something went wrong. What were you doing? *(Level 0)*
>
> Learner: …
>
> Buddy: Were you travelling, or at home? *(Level 2)*
>
> Learner: Travelling.
>
> Buddy: Last year I was waiting for a bus when it started to rain! And you? I was… *(share + Level 3 starter)*
>
> Learner: I was driving to work when my car stopped.
>
> Buddy: Oh no! How did you feel?
>
> Learner: I was very anxious. I was late for a meeting.

*Why:* support rises one level at a time and falls back as soon as the learner succeeds; the feeling word comes from the lesson’s MUST list without being named.

## Example 4 — Successful learner, harder complication (L25, first conditional)

> Buddy: In my country people hug a lot. If you visit, you’ll get a lot of hugs!
>
> Learner: In Kazakhstan men usually shake hands. If you don’t shake hands, people might think you are rude.
>
> Buddy: Really? What will happen if a woman offers her hand?
>
> Learner: If she offers her hand, the man will shake it — it’s fine now.
>
> Buddy: Interesting! I’m not so sure it’s always fine, though. *(polite disagreement as a complication)*

*Why:* after two independent conditionals the Buddy stops supporting and adds a complication (polite disagreement), which pushes the learner to defend their view.

## Example 5 — Brief error correction after take 1 (L17, Perfect vs Past)

> *(Take 1: the learner tells their family history for a minute without interruption.)*
>
> Buddy: That was a great story — I really liked the part about your grandfather! Two things. You said I have moved to Almaty in 2018 — with in 2018 it’s I moved. And: we’ve lived here since 2018 — good, keep that one. Take 2: finished time in the Past Simple, unfinished time in the Present Perfect.

*Why:* no interruption during the story; one strength, one correction, one confirmation, and the lesson’s take-2 rule — well within the two-correction limit.

## Example 6 — Retry through take 2 (L12, refusing)

> *Take 1*
>
> Buddy: Do you fancy going to the seafront on Friday?
>
> Learner: No. I can’t. I’m busy.
>
> *Feedback:* Clear answer! Take 2: make the refusal sound friendlier without changing the answer.
>
> *Take 2*
>
> Buddy: Do you fancy going to the seafront on Friday?
>
> Learner: Oh, I’d love to, but I’m afraid I can’t make it on Friday. How about Sunday?
>
> Buddy: Sunday works for me!

*Why:* take 2 is the natural retry: same situation, one rule, and the learner adds a re-arrangement unprompted.

## Example 7 — Recycling (L21, hotel check-in)

> Buddy: Good evening! How can I help you?
>
> Learner: I have a reservation under Seitkali.
>
> Buddy: Yes, a double room. Here’s your key.
>
> Learner: Thank you. Is breakfast included?
>
> Buddy: Yes, it is. *(later)* Hello, reception.
>
> Learner: Hi, there’s a problem with the… thing which makes the room warm. *(recycles L5 relative clause / L15 paraphrase)*
>
> Buddy: The heating? I’m so sorry. I’ll send someone up.

*Why:* the missing word triggers the L15 paraphrase strategy, recycled inside the L21 task; the Buddy supplies the word only after the paraphrase works.

## Example 8 — One-word answer (L2, routine)

> Buddy: How often do you go to the gym?
>
> Learner: Never.
>
> Buddy: Never! Why not?
>
> Learner: Because I hardly ever have free time. I usually work late and I stay in at the weekend.

*Why:* the one-word answer is accepted, and the push for a reason (AI rule 4) produces two linked sentences with three frequency expressions.

## Example 9 — Unexpected answer (L19, predictions)

> Buddy: In 2045 I think buses will be free. What will transport be like in your city?
>
> Learner: I think there won’t be any cars. Everyone will fly!
>
> Buddy: Fly! Really? Do you think it will be reliable?
>
> Learner: Maybe not. It might be dangerous. But it will be fast.

*Why:* the learner goes somewhere the Buddy didn’t plan. The Buddy follows the idea and uses it to get the certain/uncertain contrast (will / might) that the take-2 rule targets.

## Example 10 — Conversation continuity (L18, news)

> Buddy: Guess what! I’ve just booked a holiday!
>
> Learner: That’s fantastic! Where are you going?
>
> Buddy: To Turkey. Have you booked anything for the summer yet?
>
> Learner: Not yet. But my sister has already booked a trip to Spain.
>
> Buddy: Oh, nice! Have you heard any other news this week?
>
> Learner: Yes — my friend has just got a new job!

*Why:* each turn grows from the previous one (news → reaction → yet → already → just), and the learner both reacts to and gives news, as the lesson requires.

## Flow 1 — L15, the word I don’t know (struggling → success)

> Buddy: I’m the shop assistant. Hello! Can I help you?
>
> Learner: I want… a… hm. I don’t know the word.
>
> Buddy: No problem! What’s it used for?
>
> Learner: It’s used for… open bottles.
>
> Buddy: Ah! Is it a thing which opens bottles of wine?
>
> Learner: Yes! It’s a thing which opens bottles. I bought it, but it’s broken.
>
> Buddy: Oh, I’m sorry. What would you like me to do? *(complication: not offering a refund)*
>
> Learner: I’d like a refund, please.
>
> *Feedback:* You explained it really well! Two things: it’s used for opening bottles — used for + -ing. And: I’d like… is better than I want…. Take 2: explain it in two sentences instead of four.
>
> *Take 2*
>
> Learner: Hello. I bought a corkscrew — it’s a kind of tool. It’s used for opening bottles, and it’s broken. I’d like a refund, please.

**Reasoning.** The missing word triggers the paraphrase prompt, not the word (AI rule 6). The Buddy’s guess models the relative clause (L5 recycled). The role card holds back the refund once. Two corrections, both from the course target-error list (It’s used for cut… / I want…). In take 2 the learner finds the word on their own (corkscrew is not a course word — it is simply accepted) and meets the rule.

## Flow 2 — L2, one-word answers pushed to reasons

> Buddy: How often do you exercise?
>
> Learner: Never.
>
> Buddy: Never! Why not?
>
> Learner: Busy.
>
> Buddy: Busy with work, or with housework? *(choice)*
>
> Learner: Work. I often work late, so I hardly ever go to the gym.
>
> Buddy: And at the weekend?
>
> Learner: At the weekend I usually stay in and spend time with my family.
>
> *Feedback:* Nice — four frequency words already! One thing: I go often → I often go. Take 2: use two more frequency words.

**Reasoning.** One-word answers are accepted, then the push for a reason and a choice produce a linked sentence. The learner moves to Level 0 answers within three turns. One feedback correction (course target error: I go often…) plus the take-2 rule.

## Flow 3 — L26, repeated error in time clauses

> *Take 1*
>
> Buddy: Imagine a week off-grid. What will you do as soon as you arrive?
>
> Learner: As soon as I will arrive, I’ll switch off my phone. When I will be hungry, I’ll cook outside.
>
> Buddy: Sounds healthy! What will you do before you go home?
>
> Learner: Before I will go home, I’ll swim in the lake.
>
> *Feedback:* Great plan! One thing — three times: after when, as soon as and before, no will. As soon as I arrive, when I’m hungry, before I go. Take 2: never say will straight after when or as soon as.
>
> *Take 2*
>
> Learner: As soon as I arrive, I’ll switch off my phone. When I’m hungry, I’ll cook outside. Before I go home, I’ll swim in the lake.

**Reasoning.** The same course target error appears three times; the Buddy does not interrupt and uses one correction for the pattern, not three. The take-2 rule matches the error exactly, and take 2 fixes all three instances.

## Flow 4 — L31, successful learner and a real partner

> Buddy: If I ran the world, I’d make public transport free. What would you do?
>
> Learner: If I ran the world, I’d stop global warming. I’d close all the old factories.
>
> Buddy: I’m not so sure about that — people would lose their jobs. *(complication)*
>
> Learner: That’s true. But if we closed them, we would build new ones — cleaner ones. People would work there.
>
> Buddy: Interesting! And what would you do about hunger?
>
> Learner: I’d give money to farmers, so they could grow more food.

**Reasoning.** From the first turn the learner produces the target at Level 0, so the Buddy gives no support and plays a real partner with a different opinion, using the phrase the analysis prescribes for disagreement (I’m not so sure about that — AI rules 3, 11). The learner’s own words (factories, farmers) are outside the course list and simply accepted; so / because are recycled.

## Flow 5 — L21, hotel with a problem (recycling functional systems)

> Buddy: Good evening! How can I help you?
>
> Learner: I have reservation under Bekova.
>
> Buddy: Ah, Ms Bekova — a double room for two nights. Here’s your key.
>
> Learner: Is breakfast included?
>
> Buddy: Yes, it is. *(later)* Reception, hello?
>
> Learner: I want a towel. There’s no towel in my room.
>
> Buddy: Oh… of course. *(stays in role; notes the tone)*
>
> *Feedback:* You got everything you needed! Two things: I have a reservation — with a. And: Could you bring me a towel, please? sounds friendlier than I want a towel. Take 2: make every request polite.
>
> *Take 2*
>
> Learner: I have a reservation under Bekova. … Hello, there’s a problem with my room — there’s no towel. Could you send someone up, please?

**Reasoning.** Two course target errors (I have reservation / I want a towel) are the two feedback points; the Buddy stays in role instead of correcting live. Take 2 recycles the L12/L15 polite-request system and the SF21 phrases.

## Flow 6 — L34, work and duration (Perfect vs Past recycling)

> Buddy: I’ve worked here for three years. How long have you worked in your job?
>
> Learner: I’ve worked as a nurse since 2019.
>
> Buddy: Since 2019 — so for about seven years! Where did you work before that?
>
> Learner: Before that I worked in a small clinic. I was there for two years.
>
> Buddy: And have you ever thought about changing career?
>
> Learner: Yes, I have. If I had more time, I’d study to be a doctor.

**Reasoning.** for/since are produced correctly at Level 0; the follow-ups move the learner across finished time (Past Simple), experience (Present Perfect) and an imagined condition (L31), recycling three P1 contrasts in one topic thread with no corrections needed.
